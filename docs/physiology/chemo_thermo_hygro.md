## 1. Olfactory receptor neurons

**Spontaneous rates.** Averaged over the antennal repertoire an ORN fires ~**8 spikes/s** with no odour present (Wilson, 2013, *Annu. Rev. Neurosci.*). Per-class values scatter widely around that: maxillary palp pb1A sits at **12.8 ± 2.6 spikes/s** (Nagel & Wilson, 2011, *Nat. Neurosci.*), while trichoid T1 (Or67d) is essentially silent at **0.12 ± 0.04 spikes/s** (van der Goes van Naters & Carlson, 2007, *Curr. Biol.*). That ~100-fold spread matters: pheromone channels are near-zero-baseline detectors, generalist food channels are noisy ones. Spontaneous rate is not incidental — it sets the gain of the downstream PN (Joseph et al., 2012, *J. Neurosci.*).

**Dose–response.** ORNs saturate around **250–300 spikes/s** (Hallem & Carlson, 2006, *Cell*; ceiling quoted in Wilson, 2013, *Annu. Rev. Neurosci.*). A single ORN class has a dynamic range of only about **two orders of magnitude in odorant concentration** (Wilson, 2013). Hallem & Carlson screened 24 receptors against >100 odours in the "empty neuron" (Δhalo ab3A) system: strong responses are sparse, tuning runs from narrow to broad, and **inhibitory responses (firing driven below baseline) are widespread** — a receptor can be suppressed to near 0 spikes/s by a non-ligand. A rate-coded drive that only ever goes up is therefore wrong in an identifiable way.

**Kinetics and adaptation.** Transduction latency (LFP) is **<10 ms**; the LFP 10–90% rise is **28–32 ms**; the transduction filter has a half-width of ~**105 ms** and low-passes at ~6 Hz. Spike generation adds a **biphasic, differentiating filter** that is stereotyped across odours and cells and is set by the balance of intrinsic Na⁺ conductances (Nagel & Wilson, 2011, *Nat. Neurosci.*). Net effect: firing peaks in ~**30 ms** and can terminate within ~**200 ms** of odour offset (Wilson, 2013). Adaptation slows response onset and shifts the concentration–response curve rightward; onset rate scales with concentration while offset rate does not (Nagel & Wilson, 2011).

**Gain control in plumes.** ab3A held at a low mean stimulus spans **0–60 Hz**, but as the mean odour concentration is raised tenfold the neuron keeps its **mean rate near ~30 Hz** by scaling gain as an approximate power law with exponent **−1** (Weber–Fechner). A separate, faster variance-gain mechanism changes gain by a factor of ~**1.3** with a timescale of ~**130 ms** (Gorur-Shandilya et al., 2017, *eLife*). Crucially, **firing-rate lags stay roughly invariant with concentration while LFP lags grow** — the spiking stage preserves plume timing that transduction smears.

**Plume statistics.** Whiff durations and blank (no-odour) durations in natural plumes are both distributed as power laws with exponent **−3/2**, as are whiff intensities (Gorur-Shandilya et al., 2017, *eLife*, following Celani et al., 2014, *Phys. Rev. X*). Concretely, moving away from a source does *not* smoothly lower the ORN rate; it lowers **intermittency** (fraction of time in contact) and leaves whiff *amplitude* broadly distributed. A fly 20 cm downwind still sees occasional near-source-strength whiffs, just rarely. Any distance→rate map that is monotone and smooth throws away the signal the fly actually navigates on.

**Named channels.**
- **Or67d** (at1/T1, DA1 glomerulus): cVA. Threshold ~**10⁻⁴ ng** on the stimulus cartridge; **>200 spikes/s at 5 ng cVA**; near-zero baseline (van der Goes van Naters & Carlson, 2007, *Curr. Biol.*). Required for cVA's acute effects on courtship (Kurtovic et al., 2007, *Nature*). Termination is fast and requires SNMP1 (Li et al., 2014, *PLoS Genet.*).
- **Or65a/b/c** (at4/T2, DL3): also cVA but needs **~100× more** of it, and drives a slow, prolonged response; prolonged Or65a activation suppresses aggression and courtship, and in mated females Or65a activity inhibits the DA1 glomerulus and abolishes cVA attraction (van der Goes van Naters & Carlson, 2007, *Curr. Biol.*; Liu et al., 2011, *Nat. Neurosci.*; Lebreton et al., 2014, *Sci. Rep.*). Or67d = acute/phasic cVA line, Or65a = slow/integrating cVA line.
- **Or47b** (at4A, VA1v): methyl laurate and cuticular fatty acids incl. palmitoleic acid; fly-extract responses in the **50–200 spikes/s** band (van der Goes van Naters & Carlson, 2007, *Curr. Biol.*; Dweck et al., 2015, *PNAS*). Sensitivity is **age- and juvenile-hormone-dependent**: 7-day-old males respond far more strongly than 2-day-olds across the whole dose–response; methoprene sensitises young males, precocene desensitises old ones (Lin et al., 2016, *Neuron*).
- **Or88a** (at4C, VA1d): methyl laurate, methyl myristate, methyl palmitate; necessary and sufficient for attraction in **both sexes** — it is the aggregation channel, not the courtship channel (Dweck et al., 2015, *PNAS*).
- **Ir84a** (ac4, VL2a): phenylacetaldehyde / phenylacetic acid — **food-derived**, not fly-derived — and it promotes male courtship, providing a food-context gate on mating (Grosjean et al., 2011, *Nature*). IR-expressing coeloconic ORNs are Orco-independent and generally lower-firing and less phasic than Or ORNs **[not measured — inferred from published traces; treat as slower, weaker]**.

## 2. Contact chemoreception and pheromones

**Which neurons.** Foreleg tarsal *fruitless*⁺ GRNs expressing **ppk23** and **ppk29** are required for normal male courtship; they respond to male or female cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs), and *ppk23* mutant males have a blunted behavioural response to **7,11-heptacosadiene (7,11-HD)** (Thistle et al., 2012, *Cell*). ppk23⁺ foreleg GRNs respond to 7,11-HD, and their artificial activation drives male–male courtship even without perfuming (Toda et al., 2012, *Cell Rep.*). ppk23 is also required behaviourally in an independent study (Lu et al., 2012, *PLoS Genet.*), and **ppk25** marks the subset that stimulates male courtship and female receptivity (Vijayan et al., 2014, *PLoS Genet.*).

**Two opposed populations.** Kallman et al. (2015, *eLife*) split them functionally: **F cells** (ppk25⁺, vGlut⁺) tuned to female pheromones and **excitatory** onto P1; **M cells** (ppk25⁻) tuned to male pheromones and **inhibitory** onto P1. Clowney et al. (2015, *Neuron*) show gustatory and olfactory pheromone channels converge on P1 and that **inhibition is evoked even by courtship-promoting pheromones**, tempering P1 gain. Modelling note: the sim's F-responsive / M-responsive split by wiring is right in kind — but both should be driven on contact, with the *balance* setting P1 output, not one channel silent.

**Bitter-line pheromone detection.** **Gr32a** detects 7-tricosene (7-T) through a bitter-type pathway and mediates male–male courtship suppression (Lacaille et al., 2007, *PLoS ONE*); **Gr33a** is a broad co-receptor for aversive compounds including CHC-mediated courtship inhibition (Moon et al., 2009, *Curr. Biol.*); **Gr68a** foreleg neurons are required for male courtship (Bray & Amrein, 2003, *Neuron*).

**Rates.** This is the weakest quantitative area. CHC extracts elicit **dose-dependent** firing over **10⁻¹⁰ to 1 fly/ml** in the L2 neuron of i- and l-type labellar sensilla; 7-T gives a clear response in i- and s-type but **not** l-type sensilla; a male-specific tarsal sensillum (m4ms) responds to male extract with small-amplitude spikes (Inoshita, Martin, Marion-Poll & Ferveur, 2011, *PLoS ONE*). Absolute spikes/s for **tarsal ppk23⁺ CHC neurons are not published** — Thistle 2012, Toda 2012, Kallman 2015 and Clowney 2015 are all GCaMP. **[not measured]** Treat a 150 Hz tonic drive as an upper-bound placeholder; the labellar bitter-GRN ceiling of ~60 spikes/s (§3) is a better-grounded scale for a contact GRN.

**Phasic or tonic?** CHCs are non-volatile and stay on the cuticle, so there is no stimulus decay during contact — but the calcium responses in all four studies above rise and decay with contact, i.e. the *stimulus* is phasic even if the neuron is not. A tap is an event, not a step.

**What one tap conveys.** Kohatsu et al. (2011, *Neuron*) tethered males and touched a single foreleg tarsus to a female abdomen: that single contact produced a transient Ca²⁺ rise in P1 neurites and was sufficient to release the full stereotyped courtship sequence. So **one tap is a complete behavioural trigger**, not an evidence increment.

**Sex differences.** The ppk23⁺ *fru*⁺ population is male-enriched and the foreleg carries more chemosensory bristles in males than females (Possidente & Murphey, 1989, *Dev. Biol.*; Bray & Amrein, 2003, *Neuron*) — exact ratios vary by report **[verify before hard-coding]**. Feminisation of ppk23⁺ neurons alters male mating decisions (Pavlou et al., 2014, *Biol. Open*), so the dimorphism is in the neurons themselves, not only downstream.

## 3. Gustation

- **Sugar (Gr5a, Gr64a-f, Gr61a).** L-type labellar sensilla are the most sugar-sensitive class; control flies give ~**64–65 spikes/s to 100 mM sucrose** in L4/L6 (consistent with the dose–response series of Hiroi et al., 2002, *Zool. Sci.*). Gr5a covers trehalose/sucrose/glucose/maltose; the Gr64 cluster covers the rest (Dahanukar et al., 2007, *PNAS*; Jiao et al., 2007, *Curr. Biol.*). Responses are measured in a **50–1050 ms** window post-contact because of pronounced **spike-frequency adaptation** within the first second; HisCl1 suppresses that adaptation at high sugar concentrations (bioRxiv, 2024). Strong phasic-tonic adaptation of sugar GRNs is classical **[other insect: blowfly, Dethier, 1976, *The Hungry Fly*; Gillary, 1966, *J. Gen. Physiol.*]**.
- **Bitter (Gr66a, Gr33a, Gr89a…).** Peak observed across 16 bitter compounds × 31 sensilla was **60.8 ± 3.3 spikes/s** (10 mM caffeine, S5). Four functional classes: S-a/S-b broadly tuned (9–15 compounds >10 spikes/s), I-a/I-b narrow (3–7 compounds >10 spikes/s). Latencies ~**43–81 ms**, with several compounds (coumarin, saponin, escin, gossypol) delayed **>100 ms**. Responses are largely sustained over the 500 ms scoring window (Weiss et al., 2011, *Neuron*). L-type sensilla are essentially bitter-blind.
- **Water (ppk28).** ppk28 is an osmosensitive DEG/ENaC; hypo-osmolarity activates water GRNs, loss of ppk28 abolishes both the cellular and behavioural water response, and ectopic ppk28 confers water sensitivity on bitter GRNs (Cameron et al., 2010, *Nature*; Chen et al., 2010, *J. Neurosci.*). Rate-vs-osmolarity curves are not tabulated **[not measured]**; drive off `is_water_contact` as a binary.
- **Salt.** Low salt is attractive via **IR76b**, a Na⁺-permeable leak channel; loss of IR76b flips low salt to aversive without touching the aversive pathway. Spike frequencies were measured from L4 and S6 sensilla across NaCl concentrations (Zhang, Ni & Montell, 2013, *Science*). High salt is aversive via **Gr66a** bitter GRNs plus glutamatergic **ppk23** GRNs (Zhang et al., 2013, *Science*; Jaeger et al., 2018, *Nat. Commun.*). Note the collision with §2: ppk23 labels both a high-salt class and the CHC class — same gene, different neurons.
- **Pharyngeal GRNs.** Labral/LSO/VCSO neurons (Gr43a, Ir60b, Ir94e…) monitor ingested food and gate continued swallowing rather than initiating PER (LeDue et al., 2015, *Nat. Commun.*; Chen & Dahanukar, 2017, *Genetics*).
- **Behavioural threshold, in spikes.** No paper gives "N spikes → PER". The usable constraints: bitter compounds need **>10 spikes/s** from I-a sensilla to be behaviourally aversive (Weiss et al., 2011, *Neuron*); optogenetic activation of Ir94e or bitter GRNs suffices to block PER to 50 mM sucrose (Jaeger et al., 2018 / Chen & Dahanukar, 2017 lineage of work); PER threshold to sucrose shifts with satiety and with OA-VPM4 activity (Youn et al., 2018, *PLoS ONE*). Treat **~10 spikes/s sustained for ≥100 ms in one GRN class** as the behavioural detection floor **[inferred]**.

## 4. Thermosensation

**Periphery.** Each arista carries **3 warming-responsive (Hot) cells and 3 cooling-responsive (Cold/Cooling) cells**, projecting to two adjacent glomeruli in the posterior antennal lobe (Gallio et al., 2011, *Cell*; Frank et al., 2015, *Nature*). Hot cells express **Gr28b.d** (Ni et al., 2013, *Nature*); Cooling cells require **IR21a + IR25a + IR93a**, which also specify their cilium morphology (Budelli et al., 2019, *Neuron*).

**Hot cells — tonic, absolute-temperature coding.** Steady-state rates: **17 ± 3 Hz at 20 °C**, **37 ± 6 Hz at 25 °C**, **74 ± 17 Hz at 30 °C**; **Q₁₀ ≈ 4.4** (Budelli et al., 2019, *Neuron*). That is ~**5.7 Hz/°C** over 25–30 °C and ~**4 Hz/°C** over 20–25 °C — usefully modelled as exponential, not linear. Superimposed on the tonic level is a phasic component: transient increase on warming, decrease on cooling. Gr28b.d-dependent activation is reported above ~**25 °C** (Ni et al., 2013, *Nature*).

**Cooling cells — purely phasic, no absolute coding.** Baseline is **high and temperature-independent**: **92 ± 19 Hz at 25 °C** and **102 ± 20 Hz at 30 °C** (not significantly different). Sensitivity is extreme: **>50% rate increase after a ~0.1 °C drop**, **~3× baseline after ~0.2 °C**, peaking ~**0.65 s** after cooling onset and then **adapting back toward baseline while the cooling ramp is still running**. Warming transiently *inhibits* them. They behave the same above and below the 25 °C preferred point, which is why Budelli et al. argue for renaming them "Cooling Cells" (Budelli et al., 2019, *Neuron*). **They are a differentiator, not a thermometer.** A model that sets their rate from absolute temperature is wrong; set it from dT/dt around a ~95 Hz rest.

**Second order.** Thermosensory PNs split into **slow-adapting** (track magnitude of a temperature change) and **fast-adapting** (transient ON and OFF, track temporal structure); Frank et al. identified **6 fast-adapting types, 4 cooling-activated and 2 warming-activated**, plus a broadly tuned population responding to both heating and cooling that is required for normal avoidance of both hot and cold. Tuning criterion was ≥50% ΔF/F to a rapid ~2–4 °C ΔT (Frank et al., 2015, *Nature*). Persistent heat is handled by a separate "thermometer" circuit (Alpert et al., 2022, *Curr. Biol.*).

**Connectome mapping (important caution).** In FAFB, per hemisphere: **VP2 = hot** (arista, Gr28b.d⁺, 3–4 neurons); **VP3 = cold** (arista, Ir21a⁺, 4–5); **VP4 = dry** (sacculus chambers I+II, Ir40a⁺, 14); **VP5 = humid** (chamber II, Ir68a⁺, 7–9); **VP1d** ~7 (chamber II, putative evaporative cooling); **VP1l** ~7 (chamber I, *possibly cool/cooling*); **VP1m** ~7 (chamber I, *possibly humid*). 89 VP projection neurons in 38 morphological types; average sensory-neuron count per VP glomerulus is only ~¼ that of an olfactory glomerulus (Marin et al., 2020, *Curr. Biol.*). **The sim currently calls VP1m thermosensory and VP1l hygrosensory; Marin's functional inference is the reverse for both.** Those prefixes are annotation guesses, not measurements — flag rather than trust.

**Behaviour.** Preferred temperature ~**25 °C**; two-choice assays score 25 °C against tests from **10–40 °C in 5 °C steps** (Frank et al., 2015, *Nature*; Sayeed & Benzer, 1996, *PNAS*). Rapid avoidance is driven by steep gradients: in a **>5 °C/cm** gradient between ~25.5 °C and ~31.0 °C, flies segregate within **1 min** (Ni et al., 2013, *Nature*). Warm avoidance (25 vs 31 °C) depends strongly on IR-mediated Cooling Cells and only partially on Gr28b; cool avoidance (25 vs 16 °C) depends on IR21a/25a/93a (Budelli et al., 2019, *Neuron*). Per-degree turning-rate functions are not tabulated in these papers **[not measured]**.

## 5. Hygrosensation

**Cells and receptors.** Sacculus chambers I and II house a triad: **dry cells** (Ir40a, Ir93a, Ir25a), **moist cells** (Ir68a, Ir93a, Ir25a), and a third population. Ir40a is selectively hygrosensory; Ir93a and Ir25a serve both humidity and temperature (Enjin et al., 2016, *Curr. Biol.*; Knecht et al., 2016, *eLife*). Ir68a moist cells complete the triad and form their own PAL glomerulus (Frank et al., 2017, *Curr. Biol.*; Knecht et al., 2017, *eLife*). Obp59a is required for normal humidity response (Sun et al., 2018, *eLife*).

**Rates.** Moist cells increase and dry cells decrease when humidity is switched **7% → 90% RH**, and both are explicitly **non-adapting** (Knecht et al., 2017, *eLife*). These are GCaMP measurements; **no spikes/s numbers exist for fly hygroreceptors** **[not measured]**. For scale, insect hygroreceptor spike rates are typically tens of Hz with antagonistic moist/dry pairs **[other insect: Tichy & Kallina, 2010, *J. Neurophysiol.* (stick insect); Tichy, 2003, *J. Comp. Physiol. A* (cockroach)]**.

**Behaviour.** Species differ sharply: *D. mojavensis* prefers very low RH, *D. teissieri* the highest, *D. melanogaster* intermediate (Enjin et al., 2016, *Curr. Biol.*). State flips the sign: **sated flies are dry-seeking; desiccated-and-starved flies become moist-seeking and settle at 65–70% RH at 23 °C** (Enjin et al., 2016, *Curr. Biol.*; Giri, Nagloo & Enjin, 2024, *J. Exp. Biol.*). Desiccated/starved flies walk **1.7 mm/s at 20–30% RH vs 0.98 mm/s above 60% RH** (Δ ≈ 0.75 mm/s), while sated flies hold ~**0.91 mm/s** regardless of RH; mean displacement from arena centre **205 mm (desiccated/starved) vs 58 mm (sated)**; Cohen's *d* = 1.12 for the desiccated-starved preference. *Ir93a* mutants show **no** RH preference in either state (Giri et al., 2024, *J. Exp. Biol.*).

## 6. Interactions a modeller would not predict

1. **Hunger raises ORN gain presynaptically, channel-selectively.** Starvation lowers insulin signalling, de-represses **sNPFR1** transcription in **Or42b** ORNs, and sNPF then facilitates their presynaptic terminals; expressing that facilitation in fed flies mimics starvation behaviour (Root et al., 2011, *Cell*). Not global: starvation simultaneously *suppresses* the aversive **Or85a** channel via tachykinin/DTKR — appetitive channels up, aversive channels down (Ko et al., 2015, *eLife*).
2. **Octopamine acts centrally on state, not on the ORN.** Blocking octopamine in SEZ octopaminergic neurons abolishes starvation-induced hyperactivity but not increased consumption (Yu et al., 2016, *eLife*); OA also shifts the PER threshold via OA-VPM4 (Youn et al., 2018, *PLoS ONE*). Model OA as a locomotor/threshold gain term, not an ORN rate multiplier.
3. **Juvenile hormone sensitises a pheromone channel with age.** Or47b ORN sensitivity to palmitoleic acid rises from day 2 to day 7 and is bidirectionally controlled by methoprene/precocene (Lin et al., 2016, *Neuron*). A 2-day-old and a 7-day-old male are different sensory animals.
4. **Thermo and hygro converge before the PAL is left.** Hot, cold, dry and humid glomeruli sit adjacent in the posterior antennal lobe, and at least one PN type takes direct synaptic input from **both** a temperature and the dry-air glomerulus. Behaviourally, flies avoid **dry heat more strongly than humid heat**, and that modulation disappears when dry-air receptors are silenced (Frank et al., 2017, *Curr. Biol.*). Temperature avoidance must read humidity.
5. **Olfactory neurons are also humidity sensors.** **Or42b** ORNs respond directly to humidity through cuticular deformation of their sensillum, requiring the mechanosensitive channel **TMEM63**, and are needed for moisture attraction (Li, Li, Gao, Wang & Yan, 2022, *Nat. Commun.*). The food-odour channel and the humidity channel are the same neurons.
6. **Pheromone and vision converge on P1.** A single foreleg tap evokes a P1 Ca²⁺ transient and releases courtship (Kohatsu et al., 2011, *Neuron*); gustatory and olfactory pheromone pathways converge on P1 with both excitation and inhibition (Clowney et al., 2015, *Neuron*); and P1-driven arousal **selectively raises the gain of LC10a visual projection neurons**, so the same moving target is only pursued in the aroused state (Hindmarsh Sten et al., 2021, *Nature*). Pheromone drive does not add to visual drive — it multiplies it.
7. **Mating status rewires the female's cVA response.** Post-mating, high cVA exposure drives **Or65a**, which inhibits the DA1 glomerulus and abolishes cVA attraction (Lebreton et al., 2014, *Sci. Rep.*); feeding state further modulates female pheromone attraction and receptivity (Lebreton et al., 2015, *Sci. Rep.*).
8. **Ambient temperature changes courtship itself.** Copulation latency and duration both vary systematically across **18–28 °C**, and conspecific mate choice is contingent on mating temperature (*Anim. Behav.*, 2025, "Temperature affects conspecific and heterospecific mating rates in *Drosophila*"). Courtship parameters are not temperature-invariant constants.
9. **Humidity changes walking.** See §5 — desiccated flies nearly double walking speed in dry air relative to humid air (Giri et al., 2024, *J. Exp. Biol.*), an effect that lives in the motor layer, not the sensory one.

**Three changes with the largest behavioural payoff, in order.**
1. Cooling cells: rest at ~95 Hz and drive from **−dT/dt**, with ~0.65 s to peak and adaptation back to rest. Currently silent, and silent-at-95-Hz is a large missing tonic input to the PAL.
2. ORN drive: divide instantaneous concentration by a running mean (τ ≈ 1 s) before mapping to rate, and make the plume intermittent (power-law whiffs/blanks, exponent −3/2) rather than smoothly distance-graded.
3. Contact pheromone GRNs: make the tap a **burst**, not a tonic 150 Hz step, drive F and M channels simultaneously, and cap near 60 Hz.
